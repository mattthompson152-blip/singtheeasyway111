Singing Lessons When You Are Autistic

Most of what makes a singing lesson hard for autistic adults has nothing to do with singing. It is the room, the unpredictability, the eye contact, and the exhausting work of appearing fine while you learn.

Predictability is not a luxury

A lot of teachers pride themselves on keeping lessons spontaneous. For some students that is energising. For others it means an hour of low-level dread because you never know what is coming next.

There is no reason a lesson cannot follow a known shape. Check in, warm up, work on one thing, apply it to a song, recap. Same order, every time. The content changes, the structure does not.

If you want to know beforehand what we will cover, ask. It is a completely reasonable request and it costs me nothing.

Your camera can be off. If being watched while you sing is the thing stopping you, turn it off and I will turn mine off too. We can work like that for as long as you need. It is not a lesser lesson.

Sensory load is real and it is cumulative

Bright overhead lights, a room that echoes, a teacher who talks over your singing, a piano that is louder than you are. Any one of those is manageable. Together they use up the capacity you needed for learning.

Online removes most of it. You are in your own room, at your own volume, with your own lighting. You can turn the brightness down. You can sit rather than stand. Nobody is standing behind you.

That is not a compromise. For a lot of people it is simply a better environment to learn in.

Masking costs you the lesson

If you spend the hour managing how you appear, there is nothing left for the actual work. Nodding when you have not understood, laughing when you are overwhelmed, saying yes because saying no takes more energy than pretending.

You do not have to do any of that here. Saying I did not understand that is useful information, not a failure. So is saying I need a minute, or I would rather not do that today.

I would genuinely rather teach someone who tells me the truth about what is happening than someone who performs being fine.

I do not diagnose anything. I am a singing teacher, not a clinician, and you do not need a diagnosis or a label to book with me. If you recognise yourself in this, that is enough.

Direct instruction, not hints

A lot of teaching runs on implication. See if you can find a warmer sound. Try it with more feeling. What do you think that needs.

If that style works for you, fine. If it does not, it is not because you are missing something. I can tell you exactly what I heard, exactly what to change, and exactly how to do it, in plain terms, without you having to decode anything.

What I would do in your lesson

You would know the shape of the lesson before it starts. We would go at a pace you set, stop when you say stop, and change task rather than push through overwhelm. Nothing happens if you need a minute, and you never have to explain yourself.
