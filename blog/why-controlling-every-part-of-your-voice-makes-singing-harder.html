Why Trying to Control Every Part of Your Voice Can Make Singing Harder

First published 6 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

Learning about the voice can be useful.

Understanding that the tongue, jaw, breath, vocal folds and vocal tract all contribute to singing can help a singer make sense of a difficult problem.

But there is a point at which useful awareness becomes constant monitoring.

The singer begins trying to supervise everything.

Is the jaw released? Are the ribs expanded? Is the tongue forward? Is the larynx stable? Is the breath supported? Is the sound resonating in the correct place?

The singer may be attempting to follow every instruction faithfully.

Yet the voice becomes less responsive, not more.

Singing is a coordinated action

Singing involves many processes operating together.

That does not mean each process should be controlled consciously and separately during every phrase.

Consider walking.

Walking involves balance, joint movement, muscle activity, timing and continuous sensory information. You can study those elements, but trying to direct each muscle consciously would make normal walking more difficult.

Singing creates a similar problem.

A singer may benefit from understanding one relevant part of the coordination. Attempting to control every part simultaneously can overwhelm the task.

The goal of technical work is not permanent surveillance.

The goal is to help a more useful coordination become sufficiently familiar that the singer can return attention to the music.

Too many instructions can compete

A singer may receive several technically reasonable instructions:

  • keep the ribs open;
  • release the jaw;
  • lift the soft palate;
  • keep the tongue forward;
  • support the phrase;
  • maintain the placement;
  • avoid tension;
  • shape the vowel;
  • communicate the lyric.

Each instruction may have been given for a reason.

The problem appears when the singer tries to perform all of them at once.

The instructions begin competing for attention. The singer cannot tell which decision matters most, which one has already happened naturally or which one is making the problem worse.

The result can be a careful but restricted performance.

The singer may start managing sensations instead of sound

Sensations can help a singer recognise a useful result.

They can also become misleading when treated as fixed technical targets.

A sensation such as vibration near the front of the face may accompany a particular sound. Trying to recreate the sensation directly does not guarantee that the same coordination will appear.

The singer may start adjusting the jaw, tongue or volume in pursuit of the remembered feeling.

The original musical objective becomes secondary.

I use sensation as information, not proof.

If a personal sensation helps a singer repeat a useful sound, it may be valuable. If chasing that sensation creates additional effort, we need another reference.

More anatomical detail is not always better teaching

Accurate anatomical knowledge matters for teachers.

That does not mean every singer needs a detailed anatomical instruction for every problem.

A cue can be scientifically informed without requiring the singer to control an internal structure directly.

The teacher should choose the type of instruction that helps the individual complete the task.

The most technical description is not automatically the most useful explanation.

One clear musical objective can organise several things

A useful instruction may influence several parts of the coordination without naming each one.

For example, asking a singer to send the final word clearly towards a listener may change articulation, airflow, timing and vocal intention together.

That instruction may be more usable than asking the singer to control four separate anatomical elements.

Similarly, asking for a clearer contrast between two lyrics may produce a more useful change than asking the singer to manipulate the vocal tract while trying to remember the words.

The instruction should direct the singer towards a result.

It should not create an additional performance inside the performance.

Overcontrol often appears around difficult notes

The singer may sound natural through most of the phrase.

Then the difficult note approaches.

Suddenly, every instruction is activated:

  • take a large breath;
  • open the mouth;
  • lower the jaw;
  • lift something internally;
  • engage the abdomen;
  • place the sound;
  • prepare the high note.

The movement becomes exaggerated and the phrase loses continuity.

The singer is no longer responding to the song. The singer is trying to operate the voice manually.

This is often where I simplify the task.

Rather than adding another correction, I may remove several instructions and retain one useful objective.

The right cue differs between singers

One singer may benefit from a clear vowel instruction.

Another may respond better to a sound demonstration.

Another may need a musical image, a gesture or a direct comparison between two attempts.

The best cue is not necessarily the most technical one.

It is the cue that helps the singer produce, recognise and repeat the useful result.

This is why I do not believe in giving every singer the same sequence of internal instructions.

The vocal problem may look similar while the route to understanding it is completely different.

Control and awareness are not the same

I want singers to develop awareness.

Awareness means noticing what changed, recognising when the old habit returns and understanding which decision helps.

Control can become something different.

Constant control means attempting to prevent every possible error by managing every part of the voice in advance.

That often produces rigidity.

A singer needs enough awareness to make an informed decision and enough freedom to allow the coordinated action to happen.

A useful practice experiment

Choose one short phrase that has become overloaded with instructions.

Before singing, write down every technical thought that normally appears.

Then reduce the task to one audible or musical objective.

For example:

  • keep the vowel clear through the final note;
  • allow the phrase to continue towards the last word;
  • maintain the same conversational intention;
  • sing the line towards somebody at the other side of the room.

Record one attempt.

Do not judge whether every internal structure behaved perfectly. Listen for whether the intended result became clearer and whether the phrase remained workable.

This is not a universal solution. It is a way of discovering whether too much conscious monitoring has become part of the problem.

Good teaching should reduce unnecessary complexity

The voice is complex.

The explanation does not always need to feel complex to the singer.

My role is to identify which part of that complexity matters now.

If the singer needs one specific technical distinction, I should make it clear.

If the singer is already overloaded, adding more information may demonstrate my knowledge without improving the singing.

The measure of an explanation is not how advanced it sounds.

The measure is whether the singer can use it.

Technique should eventually serve the song

Technical understanding matters because it supports musical choice.

It should eventually allow the singer to think more about:

  • the lyric;
  • the phrase;
  • the rhythm;
  • the relationship with the accompaniment;
  • the intended vocal quality;
  • the communication of the song.

If technique permanently demands all the singer’s attention, the work has not yet become sufficiently integrated.

The aim is not to stop thinking.

The aim is to place attention where it is most useful.

Your voice may not need another instruction

If your singing becomes more difficult the more carefully you control it, the answer may not be another correction.

Ask:

  • Which instruction is actually relevant to this phrase?
  • Am I trying to control a sensation rather than an audible result?
  • Does my preparation become exaggerated before difficult notes?
  • Can I describe the musical objective in one sentence?
  • Does the phrase improve when I remove some instructions?
  • Can I recognise the result without monitoring every internal movement?
