Confidence Is Not A Personality Trait

People talk about confidence as though it were eye colour. Some have it, some do not, bad luck. In singing that is simply wrong. Confidence is a by-product of competence, and competence is built in a specific order.

Confidence follows competence, not the reverse

Telling a nervous singer to believe in themselves achieves nothing, and they know it, which makes it worse.

What changes things is being able to do something today that you could not do twenty minutes ago. Not being told you can. Hearing it happen.

That is why I aim for one audible change in a first session. It is not showmanship, it is the mechanism. Belief follows evidence and it does not work in the other direction.

One change you can hear. That is what I want out of a first lesson, more than any amount of assessment. A compliment is worth nothing to somebody who has already decided they cannot sing. A difference they hear themselves is worth everything.

Why the order matters

Most people try to build confidence first and then sing. That means waiting to feel ready, and readiness never arrives because it is manufactured by doing the thing.

Sing first, badly, in a place where badly is allowed. Collect a small piece of evidence. Repeat. The feeling catches up later, considerably later than people expect, and that is normal.

Anyone who tells you to just be confident has never had to build it from nothing.

Competence has to be specific

Vague improvement does not produce confidence. I sound a bit better is not something you can lean on when you are frightened.

I can now get through that chorus without running out of air. I can hit that note eight times out of ten. I know what to do when my throat tightens. Those are load-bearing.

So the work has to be specific enough to notice, which means one thing at a time rather than general progress.

Trust is built over months, not minutes. One student worked with me for nearly two years before he could hold a harmony he had been unable to hold at the start. In one session he held it, lost it, and found his own way back. Finding his way back is the part that mattered.

Confidence is not the absence of fear

Confident performers are not people who stopped being nervous. They are people who learned what happens next and stopped being surprised by their own adrenaline.

You will still feel it. What changes is that it stops being evidence that something is wrong, and starts being the ordinary sensation of doing something that matters to you.

That is a reframe worth having, and it takes repetition rather than insight.

Building it deliberately

Choose one thing small enough to notice. Not sing better, but get through the chorus in one breath, or hit that note without pushing. Specific enough that you can tell whether it happened.

Work on it until it is reliable rather than possible. Possible is not confidence. Eight times out of ten is confidence, because you can lean on it when the adrenaline arrives.

Then bank it and pick the next one. People imagine confidence as a mood that descends. In practice it is a stack of small reliable things you have proved to yourself, and it is built in exactly the order you would expect.

What I would do in your lesson

We would start with something you can already do reasonably well, so you hear yourself sounding good early, and only then move towards the thing that frightens you. Confidence is built from evidence, so I would make sure you collect some in the first twenty minutes.
