Why You Freeze When Someone Watches You Sing

You sound fine in the kitchen. You sound fine in the car. Then one person turns to listen and something shuts down. That is not a confidence flaw, it is a well documented physical response, and it can be worked with.

What is actually happening

Being observed while doing something you care about triggers a threat response. Adrenaline, faster breathing, tighter throat, less fine motor control. Your body has decided this is dangerous and has prioritised survival over singing.

The cruel part is that every one of those changes is bad for the voice specifically. Shallow breathing removes your support. A tight throat removes your range. Shaking hands are irrelevant to most jobs and very relevant to holding a note steady.

So it is not in your head. Something genuinely changed.

Camera off is a real option. I offer it in first lessons and a good number of people take it. I will turn mine off too. Nothing about the lesson gets worse, and you get to start singing rather than spending the hour managing being seen.

Do not aim to feel calm

Most advice about performance nerves is about becoming relaxed. I think that is the wrong target and chasing it makes things worse, because now you are failing at two things.

Adrenaline is the same chemical that makes a performance exciting rather than flat. Performers who look composed are not un-aroused, they have learned to keep working while it happens.

The goal is to sing well while feeling it. Not to stop feeling it.

Work up in stages

Going straight from singing alone to singing in front of people is a jump most people fail, and each failure reinforces the fear.

Sing with your camera off, then with it on but not looking at me. Sing facing away. Sing one line rather than a song. Record yourself and listen back, which is its own kind of being observed. Each step is small enough to survive.

Confidence is built from evidence, and evidence accumulates in small pieces.

I watch the face, not just the ears. The freeze shows up in the jaw and the eyebrows before it shows up in the sound. Often I can see the moment fear arrives, which means we can work on that moment specifically rather than on nerves in general.

The audience is not doing what you think

Singers imagine the listener analysing. Was that flat, was that breathy, did she crack on the high one.

Almost nobody listens like that. They are listening for whether it moved them. They forgive enormous technical imperfection in a performance that means something, and they are bored by flawless singing that does not.

You are being judged far less harshly, and on completely different criteria, than you assume.

What to do in the moment

If it happens mid-song, do not stop to fix it. Stopping confirms to your body that something went wrong and makes the next attempt worse.

Drop your attention to something physical and specific instead. Your feet on the floor. The breath going out rather than in. The meaning of the line you are singing rather than the sound of it. Anything that moves your focus off being watched and onto the task.

It will not feel like it worked the first time. Do it enough times and the freeze gets shorter, then shallower, then occasional. That is what progress on this actually looks like, and it is unglamorous.

What I would do in your lesson

We would find where the freeze starts, which is usually earlier than you think and often before you even open your mouth. Then we would change that specific moment. General confidence advice does not work. A repeatable change in one bar does.
