The Fear Arrives Before The Note

Watch a singer approach the line they are dreading and you will see it before you hear it. The face changes. The body braces. They start pushing before they have arrived. By the time the note comes, the damage is already done.

The note is not the problem

Singers bring me high notes. It is the single most common request. What they almost never bring me is the bar before the high note, which is where the actual problem lives.

Somewhere in the previous phrase you saw it coming. You took a bigger breath than you needed. You tightened in preparation. You set your jaw. Then you arrived at the note already braced and had to force your way through.

Work only on the note and you are treating the symptom.

This is why I teach with the camera on when people are comfortable with it. I need to see you, not just hear you. The most useful information in a lesson often arrives on your face a full second before it arrives in the sound.

What I am looking for

I watch the face as much as I listen. There is a specific expression that arrives a beat or two before a dreaded phrase, and once you know it you cannot unsee it.

The eyebrows go up. The jaw sets. Sometimes the shoulders lift. Occasionally the eyes close. It is the look of somebody preparing for impact.

That moment is the one worth working on, because it is upstream of everything else that goes wrong.

Changing the approach, not the note

Often the fix has nothing to do with the difficult note at all. We change what happens two bars earlier. Less breath, not more. A vowel adjusted before the climb rather than during it. Thinking down while going up.

Singers find that last one baffling until they try it. Aiming your attention downwards for an ascending phrase stops the body reaching and lifting, which is what closes the throat.

Small adjustment, earlier than expected, different outcome.

If it hurts, we stop. Pushing through a braced high note repeatedly is how singers do lasting damage. Pain is information, not weakness, and I will end a lesson early over it.

Why this matters more than range work

Most people who want a bigger range do not have a range problem. They have an anticipation problem that appears at a predictable point and stops them going further.

Smooth that out and notes appear that were technically already available. I have watched singers gain several usable semitones in a lesson without doing a single range exercise.

The ceiling was never where they thought it was.

How to practise the approach

Take the two bars before the difficult phrase and practise only those. Not the note. Most people never do this because the note feels like the problem, so they drill the thing that was already too late.

Sing the approach and stop before the note. Repeatedly. You are teaching your body that arriving there is safe and uneventful, which is the opposite of what it currently believes.

Then add the note back once the approach is genuinely relaxed. Frequently it simply works, and singers find that slightly annoying after years of assuming they needed more power.

What I would do in your lesson

I would ask you to sing the phrase and watch where the flinch arrives. Then we would change something in the bar before rather than at the note itself. People are often surprised how quickly it shifts, because they had been attacking the wrong target for years.
