The Fear Arrives Before The Note

First published 12 September 2026 · Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

Sometimes a singer notices effort before the difficult note arrives. The lead-in is worth hearing because changes in timing, breath, vowel or expectation may begin earlier.

The difficult moment may begin before the sound

Sometimes the hardest part of a phrase is the few seconds before it starts. You may be waiting for a high note, a breath, a cue or a change in the accompaniment, and the song can feel difficult before you have sung anything. That timing matters. A concern that arrives before one particular note is not the same question as freezing throughout a performance or sounding different whenever someone watches. Start by locating the lead-in, rather than labelling the whole experience.

There may be a practical musical reason to prepare: the preceding phrase is long, the starting pitch is uncertain, the rhythm gives little time to breathe, or the next section sits differently in your range. There may also be unease about what the note will sound like. A coach can investigate the musical sequence and listen to what happens; they cannot diagnose an anxiety condition or infer a mental-health mechanism from anticipation.

Map the few beats before the note

Write down or speak the sequence leading into the moment: what you hear, where the breath falls, what the accompaniment does and how you know it is time to enter. The cue might be the end of a piano figure, a consonant, a chord change or a count. Sing the lead-in without the target note first, then add the note if you choose. This shows whether the difficulty appears in the preparation, the pitch itself or the transition between them.

For “Someone Like You” by Adele, choose a manageable passage that leads towards a sustained or exposed phrase, and adjust the key if needed. Sing the preceding bars at an easy volume, pause, then add the target only when you are ready. A coach can listen for whether the tempo rushes, the breath arrives late or the pitch is unclear before the note. The example is not a claim that the song is easy or suitable for every voice.

Separate anticipation from the vocal demand

Test the musical components at a comfortable level. Find the starting pitch on a keyboard, speak the rhythm, sing the phrase on a simple sound, or shorten the lead-in. If the phrase becomes more reliable once the cue is clear, that is useful information. If the same difficulty remains, the note or surrounding technique may need another adjustment, such as a different key or a smaller range. A contemporary coach can test these choices and listen for changes without insisting on a single explanation.

Keep one factor steady. If you change the key, tempo, accompaniment and vowel all at once, you will not know which alteration mattered. Use one attempt to check the cue, another to check breath timing, and a third—only if useful—to sing the phrase in context. You can choose a lower note or stop before the exposed moment. Musical preparation should give you options, not make the phrase into a test of nerve.

Rehearse the transition, not just the note

People often repeat the hardest note in isolation and skip the transition that sets it up. Instead, start a bar or two earlier and practise arriving at the note without forcing volume. Then try the lead-in with the accompaniment, with a count-in, or with the cue removed to see whether you can find it independently. You might finish by singing the phrase through once at a comfortable dynamic. Keep the number of repetitions modest and stop if the voice feels strained.

For “Someone Like You”, you could compare singing the phrase after a clear count with singing it after the actual accompaniment cue. If the count is helpful but the cue is not, the musical problem may be locating the entry. If both are clear but the note feels uncomfortable, discuss range or vocal coordination with a coach. The song example is a way to define the task; it is not a recommendation to push through a note that does not suit your voice.

Do not predict the whole performance from one approach

A familiar mistaken assumption is that feeling worried before a note proves the note will go wrong. Another is that the only solution is to eliminate the feeling before singing. Neither conclusion is required for a useful lesson. A singer can make a musical choice while feeling uncertain, and a well-prepared phrase may still vary from day to day. Track what you can hear and change: where the cue comes, whether the breath is ready, and what key or tempo makes the line manageable.

If fear is severe, persists beyond a particular singing task, or affects other parts of life, seek appropriate support outside voice coaching. The NHS anxiety guidance explains options for help. A singing coach can stay with musical preparation and should not present lessons as mental-health treatment or ask you to force an exposure exercise.

Make a small plan for the next arrival

Choose one target phrase and decide what would make its lead-in clearer: a marked breath, a starting pitch, a count, a slower tempo or a key change. Try it once, listen for one musical detail, and decide whether to keep it. At the next rehearsal, begin from the same cue so you can compare like with like. You can also choose to leave the target note out and work on the rest of the song.

The goal is not to guarantee that anticipation disappears. It is to make the musical events before the note understandable and give you a choice about what happens next. Record the practical result—“I found the cue” or “the phrase feels better in a lower key”—rather than scoring whether you were afraid. That distinction keeps coaching honest, specific and within scope.
