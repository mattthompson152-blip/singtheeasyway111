Pitch and musical accuracy

Your Voice Can Be Wrong Before You Make a Sound

First published 10 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

You take the breath.

You prepare the vowel.

You open your mouth and sing the note.

It comes out flat.

The obvious conclusion is that the voice failed to reach the pitch.

But what if the mistake happened earlier?

What if the note was already unclear before the vocal folds began to vibrate?

Singing in tune is not simply a matter of moving the voice upwards or downwards until a tuner displays the correct note. Before the voice produces a pitch, the singer needs some form of musical target.

That target might be extremely clear. The singer may be able to hear the note internally, anticipate its relationship with the phrase and begin singing with a strong sense of destination.

Or the target may be vague.

The singer knows that the melody goes “up”, remembers roughly what the phrase sounds like and hopes the voice will locate the note during the attempt.

Those are completely different starting points.

If the target is unclear, correcting the mouth, breath or posture may not solve the actual problem. The singer may be trying to coordinate the voice towards a note that has not yet been defined accurately enough.

Singing begins before the sound

A sung note does not begin when sound leaves the mouth.

Before that happens, the singer has already made several decisions, consciously or otherwise:

  • which pitch is coming;
  • how far it is from the current note;
  • how the note relates to the key;
  • what vowel will carry it;
  • when the note begins;
  • how loudly it should be sung;
  • what vocal quality belongs to the phrase;
  • whether the note is expected to be difficult.

The voice responds to that preparation.

If the singer anticipates a difficult leap, the body may brace early. If the pitch is remembered inaccurately, the voice may move confidently towards the wrong destination. If the target is uncertain, the singer may slide, search or add volume in an attempt to find it.

This is why pitch correction cannot always begin with the sound that eventually appeared.

Sometimes we have to examine what the singer expected to happen before the attempt began.

Hearing that a note is wrong is not the same as knowing the right note

Many singers recognise an inaccurate note during playback.

The recording begins, the phrase reaches the difficult point and the singer immediately says:

That note is flat.

That recognition is useful, but it does not prove that the singer had a clear target before singing.

There is a difference between:

  • recognising an error after it happens;
  • hearing the correct note when somebody else plays it;
  • remembering the note after the reference stops;
  • anticipating the note inside the phrase;
  • producing it accurately without searching.

A singer may be excellent at the first two and still struggle with the last three.

That does not mean the singer has no musical ear.

It means that the connection between hearing, remembering, anticipating and producing the note may need to become more dependable.

The piano can hide the real difficulty

A singer may match a note accurately while the piano is sounding.

The reference stops, and the pitch begins to drift.

This reveals an important distinction.

The singer may be following an external sound rather than maintaining an internal target.

That is not a failure. External reference is part of learning. But if the singer always depends on the note being played at exactly the same time, the skill may not survive inside an unaccompanied entrance, a recording session or a phrase where the accompaniment does not clearly provide the melody.

I sometimes test this gradually:

  1. Play the target note.
  2. Ask the singer to listen without singing.
  3. Remove the sound.
  4. Allow a short pause.
  5. Ask the singer to imagine the note.
  6. Sing it once.
  7. Compare the attempt with the reference.

The pause matters.

It reveals whether the singer can retain the target rather than merely join a sound that is already present.

A wrong note can be confidently wrong

Not every inaccurate note sounds uncertain.

A singer can produce the wrong pitch with impressive confidence, volume and consistency.

That often means the singer is not failing to control the voice. The voice may be reproducing the target it was given.

The internal target is simply not the one required by the song.

This commonly happens when:

  • the melody has been remembered incorrectly;
  • the singer has learnt from repeatedly singing along with a recording;
  • the accompaniment obscures the precise interval;
  • a harmony note has become confused with the melody;
  • the singer expects the phrase to follow a familiar pattern;
  • the lyric rhythm causes the melody to be grouped incorrectly.

More breath support will not correct a melody that has been learnt inaccurately.

Nor will repeatedly singing the complete song.

The first task is to establish the musical target clearly.

The interval matters more than the isolated note

A singer does not usually perform a collection of unrelated pitches.

Each note arrives from somewhere.

The difficulty may therefore lie in the distance between two notes rather than in the target pitch itself.

A singer might produce the target note accurately when it is played in isolation, yet miss it consistently when approaching from the previous note.

The voice can sing the destination.

The problem is navigating the route.

I might then separate the interval from the rest of the phrase:

  • sing the first note;
  • pause;
  • hear the second note internally;
  • sing the second note;
  • reverse the interval;
  • restore the original rhythm;
  • add the relevant vowel;
  • restore the complete word;
  • return the interval to the phrase.

This helps distinguish a pitch-memory problem from a vocal-coordination problem.

If the singer hears the interval clearly but the voice cannot reproduce it comfortably, we investigate the coordination.

If the interval itself remains unclear, we work on the musical target before adding more vocal instructions.

Lyrics can interfere with the target

A melody may be accurate on one sound and become unstable when the words return.

That can happen because the vocal coordination changes, but attention can also be part of the problem.

The singer starts thinking about:

  • the next word;
  • a difficult consonant;
  • breath placement;
  • the meaning of the lyric;
  • the approaching high note;
  • whether the previous note was correct.

The internal pitch target becomes less vivid because attention has moved elsewhere.

This is particularly important in fast or lyrically dense phrases. The singer may know every note when singing slowly, then lose accuracy when the original rhythm returns.

The solution is not necessarily to repeat the complete line more forcefully.

Instead, I might rebuild it through stages:

  1. Speak the words in rhythm.
  2. Sing the melody on one sound.
  3. Sing only the vowels.
  4. Restore the consonants.
  5. Perform the phrase at a slower tempo.
  6. Return gradually to the original tempo.

The aim is to preserve the musical target while restoring the competing demands of the song.

Looking at a tuner can help and hinder

A visual pitch display can provide useful information.

It can show whether a sustained pitch is consistently sharp or flat. It can help compare two attempts and make a change easier to observe.

Research has found that real-time visual feedback can improve pitch-matching performance in a controlled task. In one study involving participants without previous musical experience, groups receiving visual or certain auditory feedback improved their intonation, although the results depended on the task and feedback condition.

But the tuner is not the musical target.

If a singer watches the screen continuously, the task can become reactive:

  1. produce a sound;
  2. see where the line appears;
  3. adjust the voice;
  4. chase the display;
  5. overshoot;
  6. correct again.

The singer may eventually centre the line without developing a clear sense of the note before singing it.

A better use is often:

  • sing without watching;
  • inspect the result afterwards;
  • identify a pattern;
  • make one change;
  • sing again;
  • compare the attempts.

The screen provides evidence. It should not replace listening.

The note in your imagination matters

Musicians can experience aspects of music internally when no external sound is present. This is often described as auditory or musical imagery.

A 2024 study found that auditory-imagery ability was associated with maintaining tonal accuracy under some altered-feedback and distraction conditions. The study was preliminary and does not prove that one imagery exercise will correct every singer’s pitch, but it supports the narrower idea that internal tonal representation can matter when external feedback becomes unreliable.

For practical singing work, I am interested in whether the singer can:

  • remember the note after the reference stops;
  • imagine the beginning of the phrase;
  • anticipate the direction of the interval;
  • maintain the key during a pause;
  • notice when the produced note differs from the intended one.

Some singers describe this as hearing the note internally.

Others do not experience a vivid internal sound but still recognise its direction, relationship or musical character.

The language matters less than the function.

The singer needs a usable target before the voice is asked to reproduce it.

Do not confuse uncertainty with lack of talent

Pitch problems can become emotionally loaded very quickly.

A missed note is treated as evidence that the singer has a bad ear, lacks talent or should never sing in front of anybody.

That conclusion is far larger than the evidence.

An inaccurate note might involve:

  • an unclear target;
  • inaccurate melodic memory;
  • difficulty hearing the accompaniment;
  • an interval that has not been learnt securely;
  • a vowel that disrupts the coordination;
  • excessive volume;
  • a registration change;
  • divided attention;
  • delayed recognition of the error;
  • an unsuitable key.

Those are different problems.

They require different work.

Telling the singer simply to “listen properly” is no more useful than telling somebody to “sing better”.

More volume can make the pitch problem harder to solve

When a singer is unsure of a note, volume can create a sense of commitment.

The attempt feels more decisive.

But decisiveness is not accuracy.

Additional volume may make the voice less adjustable, particularly if the singer is already preparing the phrase with unnecessary effort.

The singer may then interpret the physical intensity as evidence that the note is correct.

I would rather hear a manageable attempt that gives us useful information than a forceful attempt that hides the distinction between hearing and coordination.

Reducing the volume temporarily can make it easier to discover:

  • whether the target is accurate;
  • whether the interval is understood;
  • whether the voice is sliding towards the note;
  • whether the vowel changes at the wrong moment;
  • whether effort increases before the pitch arrives.

The final performance may require intensity.

The investigation does not have to begin there.

The accompaniment may be misleading you

Some arrangements make the melody easy to hear.

Others do not.

The accompaniment may contain:

  • close harmony;
  • a bass note that suggests another tonal centre;
  • instruments doubling a harmony rather than the melody;
  • dense production;
  • altered chords;
  • rhythmic displacement;
  • a melodic line that disappears before the vocal entrance.

A singer may perform accurately with the original artist because the recording provides a strong vocal guide, then struggle with a karaoke track where that support is absent.

That does not necessarily mean the singer suddenly lost the melody.

It may mean the original vocal was doing more of the navigational work than the singer realised.

A useful test is to sing the phrase with:

  1. the original recording;
  2. a clear piano reference;
  3. the backing track;
  4. a reduced accompaniment;
  5. no accompaniment.

The point at which accuracy changes tells us something important.

Correct the first wrong assumption, not the final wrong note

If a phrase ends inaccurately, the instinct is to repeat the final note.

But the first error may have occurred earlier.

Perhaps the singer:

  • entered on the wrong starting pitch;
  • misheard the second interval;
  • changed key internally during a pause;
  • followed a harmony in the accompaniment;
  • altered the rhythm and shortened the preparation;
  • anticipated the final note too early.

By correcting only the last note, we may leave the original cause untouched.

I work backwards until I find the first moment where the singer’s intended melody and the actual melody separate.

That is usually the most useful place to begin.

A practical test for one inaccurate phrase

Choose one short phrase that repeatedly moves out of tune.

Do not begin by singing the whole song.

Step 1: Establish the melody

Play the phrase using a clear piano or keyboard sound.

Listen without singing.

Step 2: Remove the words

Sing the phrase on one comfortable sound at a manageable volume.

Step 3: Check the interval

Identify the exact movement where the pitch changes.

Practise only the note before and the target note.

Step 4: Create a pause

Listen to the target note, stop the reference, wait briefly and sing it once.

Step 5: Restore the vowel

Use the important vowel from the lyric without the surrounding consonants.

Step 6: Restore the words

Return the complete lyric while preserving the interval.

Step 7: Test independence

Sing the phrase without the piano doubling the melody.

This does not diagnose every pitch problem.

It gives you a method for discovering where the problem changes.

Accurate singing is prediction as well as correction

Pitch control is often described as a feedback process.

The singer produces a note, hears the result and adjusts.

That matters, but singing cannot depend entirely on correcting notes after they appear. Music moves too quickly.

The singer also needs to predict.

Before the phrase begins, the singer needs some representation of:

  • the starting note;
  • the melodic direction;
  • the important intervals;
  • the key;
  • the timing;
  • the intended quality.

The better defined that target becomes, the less the singer has to search during the sound.

Research on auditory and visual feedback in pitch matching supports the importance of feedback, while research on auditory imagery suggests that internally represented sound can also contribute to pitch stability.

Neither finding supplies a universal singing method, but together they support a practical distinction between hearing what happened and knowing what you intended to produce.

The question I ask before correcting the voice

When a singer misses a note, I do not begin with:

What is the voice doing wrong?

I begin with:

What note did you expect to sing?

That question can change the entire lesson.

If the singer’s target is accurate and clear, we investigate why the voice did not reproduce it.

If the target is vague or inaccurate, we establish the music first.

This prevents the singer from receiving a collection of physical corrections for a problem that began in musical preparation.

The wrong note may not be a vocal failure

If your pitch becomes unreliable, do not assume that the voice is simply refusing to cooperate.

Ask:

  • Can I hear the target before I sing?
  • Can I retain it after the reference stops?
  • Do I know the exact interval?
  • Does the pitch change when I add the lyric?
  • Am I following the melody or another line in the accompaniment?
  • Does accuracy change when I reduce the volume?
  • Can I identify the first incorrect note rather than only the final one?
  • Am I using the tuner as evidence or chasing it during the sound?

The note that comes out of the mouth is the end of a process.

Sometimes the most important correction belongs at the beginning.
