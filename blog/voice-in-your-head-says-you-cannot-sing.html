The Voice In Your Head That Says You Cannot Sing

Almost every adult who tells me they cannot sing can name the moment they decided it. A comment, a laugh, one teacher, one audition. Usually decades ago. Usually from somebody who was not qualified to make the call.

One moment is not a verdict

A sibling laughing in the back of a car. A music teacher moving you to the back row. A friend who said something careless at a party. None of those people assessed your voice. They reacted to one sound on one day.

But it lands as a verdict, and then you spend thirty years arranging your life so you never have to test it. You do not sing in front of people. You stop singing along in the car when someone else gets in.

The belief never gets updated because it never gets challenged.

Nobody is judged here. What you sing in the first few minutes does not define your voice, it introduces it to me, and I say so before anyone starts. You are allowed to be bad. You are allowed to forget the words.

What the inner voice actually does to the sound

This is the part people miss. The belief does not just sit there making you sad, it physically changes what comes out.

If you expect to sound bad, you brace. You sing quietly so nobody can hear the mistake clearly. You hold back the air. You tighten the throat because a small sound feels safer than a big one. All of that makes the voice worse, which confirms the belief, which makes you brace harder.

So by the time I meet you, I am not just listening to your voice. I am listening to your voice plus thirty years of protective habits stacked on top of it.

Evidence beats reassurance

Telling somebody they can sing does nothing. They have heard it from kind friends and they have discounted it, because kind friends are supposed to say that.

What works is evidence. One change, made in the lesson, that you can hear yourself. Not my opinion of your voice, your own ears telling you something just happened that you did not think was possible.

That is why I aim for at least one audible difference in a first session. Not to impress you. To start putting a dent in the story.

I see it before they do. The most common thing I say in a first lesson is that there is more in there than the person realises. That is not flattery, it is the single most reliable observation of my career.

Genuine tone deafness is rare

Actual amusia, the inability to perceive pitch relationships, affects a very small proportion of people. Far smaller than the proportion who believe they have it.

What is common is hearing pitch perfectly well and having a voice that cannot yet reliably land on it. That is coordination, and coordination is trainable. It is not the same thing at all, and confusing the two costs people years.

What I would do in your lesson

I would ask what happened, because there is usually a specific moment and naming it takes some of the power out of it. Then we would find one change you can hear. Not a compliment, a difference. Those are not the same currency and only one of them counts.
