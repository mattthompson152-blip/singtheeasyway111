Vocal identity

Finding Your Authentic Voice: Beyond Imitation

Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

It is easy to mistake imitation for progress when a familiar sound seems to offer a ready-made answer. The useful question is what you admire in it, and which parts belong to your own voice.

Influence is part of learning a style

Singers learn by listening. You may pick up a vowel shape, rhythm, accent, dynamic or phrasing choice from a performer you admire. That influence is not automatically imitation to avoid. The useful question is what you want to borrow and whether that choice works in the song, key and voice you are using. “Authentic” does not have to mean an untouched sound with no influences; it can mean making deliberate musical choices rather than trying to reproduce someone else’s entire recording.

Voice coaching is not identity therapy, and a coach should not claim to uncover a single hidden “true voice”. Voices change with repertoire, context, technique and taste. The goal can be practical: gain options, keep the qualities you value and avoid stylistic choices that feel uncomfortable or unsustainable. You decide what sounds like you and what belongs in a particular song.

Separate one stylistic choice from the full imitation

Listen to a short passage and name one feature you admire: a delayed entrance, crisp consonants, a conversational vowel, a held note or a dynamic lift. Then try that feature without copying every other element of the artist’s sound. A coach can model the choice, listen to your version and compare the musical effect. You can keep it, alter it or leave it. This approach treats influence as a toolbox rather than a pass-or-fail test of originality.

“I Will Survive” by Gloria Gaynor offers plenty of stylistic details to notice, but it is not a required song or a fixed model. Pick one phrase that suits your range and try a clear rhythmic emphasis or a change in dynamic. Then sing it in a simpler way. The contrast can reveal which element you enjoy and whether it is comfortable. If you prefer a different genre or song, make that choice; the point is to identify one learnable decision.

Check whether a choice is comfortable and repeatable

A sound may be exciting for a moment but tiring if you sustain it across a whole song. Try a stylistic idea at a modest volume and in a phrase you can comfortably repeat. Notice whether the vowel, consonant or volume change affects the voice. A contemporary coach can listen for clarity, pitch, timing and vocal effort, and suggest another way to create a similar effect. They should not prescribe a style because it is fashionable or assume that a particular sound is the singer’s natural identity.

For the Gaynor example, you could compare a stronger rhythmic attack with a lighter delivery while keeping the melody and tempo steady. If one version makes the words clearer but creates strain, ask about a smaller adjustment. If both are comfortable, choose based on the story you want the song to tell. The aim is maintainable choice, not proving that you can reproduce the intensity of a record or adopting a sound that only works for one short take.

Keep the song and the setting in view

Authenticity is not one sound that must remain identical in every genre. A singer may choose a brighter tone in an upbeat song, a quieter delivery in a reflective one, or a more speech-like quality in a story-driven passage. Those are musical decisions. Ask whether a choice supports the lyric, melody and arrangement, and whether it feels like something you want to repeat. There is no need to make a personal claim about who you are in order to shape a phrase.

When exploring “I Will Survive”, choose whether the song calls for a direct, energetic delivery or a more restrained interpretation in your arrangement. A coach can sing both options and help you hear the difference. You may borrow one part of the reference while changing another. The song can remain recognisable without your voice becoming a replica of the original artist’s timbre or production.

Notice the difference between taste and technique

Sometimes “that does not sound like me” means the style is not your preference; sometimes it points to a technical task that needs adjustment. A vowel may feel awkward, a pitch may be difficult or a phrase may be hard to sustain. Describe the audible or physical detail as specifically as you can, and choose whether you want the coach to offer another option. A teacher can help test a technique, but should not override your taste simply because a sound is possible.

Common mistaken assumptions include that copying any influence is inauthentic, that the most natural sound is always the best choice, and that technique should remove every distinctive feature. None is necessary. A technique can expand the ways you sing without erasing a quality you like. Equally, you may decide not to use a sound that feels forced. The singer’s preference is a relevant part of a contemporary coaching conversation.

Build a small repertoire of chosen sounds

Make a short list of stylistic options you enjoy: a lighter onset, a stronger consonant, a longer phrase or a more conversational rhythm. Try them in songs where they make sense and note which are comfortable and repeatable. This is not a prescription to collect techniques or to sound different for its own sake. It helps you recognise that you have choices and can select among them as the music asks.

After a lesson, you might write: “I liked the rhythmic emphasis in the first version; I prefer the softer vowel in the second.” That is more useful than “I found my authentic voice”, as it records decisions you can revisit. A coach can support musical experimentation and give feedback on what they hear. They cannot define your identity, and they should not promise that one stylistic exercise will reveal a permanent answer.

Let your version remain a version

Before singing a song, choose one influence and one personal preference. Try the borrowed feature, then try the phrase without it. Listen for what changes and whether either option supports the music. You can combine elements, switch between them or choose something different in performance. The version you make does not need to defeat the original; it needs to be singable, meaningful to you and suitable for the moment.

If a choice causes discomfort or vocal strain, stop and discuss a different approach with a qualified singing teacher; do not force it in the name of authenticity. Coaching can work with style and vocal technique, while questions about identity or emotional distress belong elsewhere if you want support with them. Your voice is not a puzzle a teacher has to solve. It is an instrument you can shape through choices that remain yours.
