Performance context

Singing with Performance Nerves

Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

Nerves can change how a familiar song feels. This article concerns musical coaching observations, not mental-health treatment or a guarantee that anxiety will disappear.

Define the performance situation first

“Stage fright” can refer to a range of experiences, from a lively rush before a familiar set to distress that makes performing feel unmanageable. The practical context matters: a first open mic, a camera audition, a planned gig and singing in front of family ask different things of you. Consider the room, sound system, audience size, order of songs and whether you can restart. There is no requirement to treat every performance as a personal challenge or to perform at all.

A 2025 review of music performance anxiety notes that anxiety may be experienced as facilitative or detrimental depending on appraisal and regulation, and argues for flexible approaches rather than one formula (review, PMID 40309210). That finding does not show that singing coaching treats anxiety; it is a reason to avoid assuming that every performer needs the same response.

This article is about musical preparation, not anxiety treatment. A voice coach can help you prepare a song, identify an uncertain transition and plan the practical set-up. They cannot diagnose or treat an anxiety condition. If distress is severe, persistent or affecting life beyond singing, contact a GP or NHS support. The NHS anxiety information describes when and where to seek help; a performance lesson should not stand in for healthcare.

Prepare the song and its context

Choose a song you can sing in a suitable key and decide what the performance needs to accomplish: tell a story, share a favourite track, or complete a short set. Plan the opening pitch, tempo, instrumental cue and ending. If you are using a microphone, try the distance and volume you expect; if there is no microphone, consider whether the room changes how much sound you need. A coach can listen for clarity, pitch, timing and vocal effort in the chosen arrangement.

“Don’t Stop Me Now” by Queen is one possible example, not a universal performance song. If you like it, choose a section that fits your range and rehearse the count-in, entry and ending with the accompaniment you plan to use. You might sing it at a lower key or choose another title entirely. The useful exercise is to practise the actual sequence—starting, delivering the section and finishing—rather than assuming familiarity with a studio track prepares you for every venue.

Practise the transitions around the set

Performance is not only the notes. You may need to walk to a microphone, wait through an introduction, find the starting pitch, recover from a missed cue or move into the next song. List the transitions that matter for your event and rehearse only the ones you want. A simple run-through can include the first count, the first phrase and the final chord, with a pause between each. If a stage simulation adds no value, do not use one.

For “Don’t Stop Me Now”, try beginning with the count-in or instrumental cue you expect to hear, then practise what you will do if the backing starts too soon. The contingency could be to wait for the next phrase, ask for a restart or stop and begin again; it depends on your event. A coach can help you make the response musically clear. Planning a recovery option is not a prediction that anything will go wrong.

Choose a preparation ladder that stays voluntary

You might practise alone, then with one chosen listener, then in the room or format you intend to use. Or you may prefer to rehearse the song technically and skip simulated audience steps. Any progression should be chosen, paced and stoppable by you; there is no rule that you must increase the audience until nerves disappear. The point is to make the musical material and practical details familiar enough for the performance you actually want to give.

A coach can ask which part of the setting you want to include and keep other conditions unchanged. For example, sing the opening with the same backing track while sitting in a different room, rather than changing the song, volume and audience at once. Notice whether the starting cue is audible and whether the tempo remains comfortable. These are observations about preparation, not a therapeutic exposure plan or evidence that a particular progression is clinically effective.

Plan recovery and limits before the event

Choose an option for ordinary problems: a forgotten word, an accompaniment that stops, or a phrase that needs another breath. Depending on the event, you might repeat a section, skip to the next cue, pause, or finish early. You can also arrange support from an organiser or friend. Decide what would make you stop; “keep going no matter what” is not a necessary performance rule. A calm, practical plan can protect your musical choices without promising a flawless outcome.

Common mistaken assumptions include that a performer must feel relaxed, that every singer needs the same warm-up or visualisation, and that one difficult event means they are not ready. Feeling nervous does not determine the quality of the music, and the result of one performance does not define your capacity. Equally, a singer should not be pressured into an event simply to build confidence. Your purpose, preferences and wellbeing remain relevant to the decision.

Use coaching for a defined musical question

Bring a specific point to a lesson: whether the key fits, whether the accompaniment gives you a clear entry, how to make the first phrase audible, or what to do after a missed cue. Ask the coach to listen for that one detail. For the Queen example, they might hear that the pulse is comfortable but the starting pitch is difficult to locate; that calls for a different preparation cue, not a judgement about stage readiness. A short, targeted rehearsal can be more useful than repeatedly performing the full song.

After the event, review what happened in practical terms. Which cue worked? Was the track loud enough? Did the ending feel clear? What would you change next time, if you choose to perform again? Avoid turning the review into a verdict on your personality or an assessment of whether you overcame fear. Coaching can refine music and preparation; health care is the appropriate route for distress that needs treatment.
