Choosing coaching

Choosing a Singing Coach: Questions Worth Asking

Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

Choosing a coach is not a contest to find the biggest list of claims. You need to understand how the person will listen to your goal, explain their choices and respond when an approach does not help.

Evaluate how they diagnose a song-specific problem

Bring a real musical moment rather than asking a prospective coach to demonstrate a generic warm-up. Explain what you hear or feel, where it occurs and what you want the line to do. For example, with ‘good 4 u’ by Olivia Rodrigo, you might ask how to keep the verse-to-chorus shift expressive without losing the words. With ‘Someone You Loved’ by Lewis Capaldi, you might ask what to examine when the chorus feels harder to repeat than the verse. These are sample questions, not a claim that one particular technique fits either song.

Notice whether the coach asks enough to understand the context: the key you are singing in, the accompaniment, the phrase before the difficulty and what you have tried. A thoughtful coach need not identify everything immediately. Listen for a process that makes its uncertainty visible: what they want to hear next, what a short test might show and how the observation would connect to the song. A confident-sounding label without a clear reason is less useful than a modest, testable explanation.

Look for feedback you can understand and question

Useful feedback points to something observable and gives you room to respond. Instead of “sing with more emotion”, a coach might identify a word or musical turn and ask what you want the listener to notice there. Instead of “use more support”, they might explain what they heard in the phrase, suggest one experiment and invite you to compare it with your usual version. The precise language can vary; what matters is that you can ask what it means and how it relates to your goal.

Ask how an exercise returns to repertoire. If a coach suggests a repeated sound or pattern, you can ask what it is meant to help you notice and how you will test it in a line from a song such as ‘Flowers’ by Miley Cyrus or ‘Stay’ by The Kid LAROI and Justin Bieber. You should not need to take an unexplained exercise on faith. It is reasonable to say that an instruction is unclear and ask for another way to describe it.

Check whether the coach adapts when a test misses

One sign of a workable coaching conversation is not that every suggestion succeeds first time; it is that the coach can respond when it does not. Ask what they would do if an adjustment made the phrase less clear, felt awkward or failed to change the issue. A useful answer may involve listening again, changing one variable, asking what you noticed or reconsidering the first interpretation. No single response proves quality, but flexibility and explanation are more informative than insisting that you must be doing it wrong.

You can make the comparison concrete. Sing the same short phrase twice under similar conditions, changing only the idea under discussion, then say which version better serves the song and why. In ‘As It Was’ by Harry Styles, for instance, the question might be whether the words remain intelligible when you alter the rhythmic emphasis. The point is not to imitate the record or reproduce a prescribed result; it is to hear whether the choice supports your own musical intention.

Make boundaries and transparency part of fit

Ask practical questions before you book or continue: what format is offered, how long the session lasts, what it costs, how payment works, what happens if you need to stop, and whether there is any expectation to buy further sessions. You should be able to understand the answer without pressure. A coach should explain what they can offer and where their role ends, rather than implying that a lesson replaces other professional support or guarantees a result.

If you mention pain, persistent voice changes or another health concern, notice whether the response stays within appropriate limits. A singing coach should not diagnose a medical condition or promise that an exercise will protect you. You can stop a test that feels wrong, ask to change the task, or seek suitable clinical advice separately. This is not a judgement about a coach’s general ability; it is a basic boundary between musical coaching and healthcare.

Separate evidence from sales language

Credentials, testimonials and a polished online profile may help you learn what a coach says about their background, but none guarantees that their approach fits your repertoire or learning preferences. Ask for a plain description of how the work is structured, what a first meeting can realistically establish and how you can decide whether to continue. Treat fixed promises about range, speed or a specific transformation cautiously; voices, repertoire, schedules and circumstances differ.

Also consider the practical delivery. If lessons are online, ask what audio and camera setup is expected and how you will handle a detail that is difficult to hear remotely. This site offers online sessions, not an in-person alternative; the article comparing online and in-room work outlines which tasks may need separate discussion. The tech setup guide explains simple checks that do not require buying a new microphone.

Decide whether the explanation fits you

After a conversation, ask yourself whether you understood what the coach heard, whether you could disagree or ask questions, and whether the proposed next step matched the song you brought. Did the feedback leave you with one useful thing to test, or a stack of instructions you could not explain? Did the person acknowledge limits and give you space to decide? These are practical fit questions, not a scorecard that can certify teaching quality from one short meeting.

You might write down the specific problem, the coach’s explanation, the test you tried and what remains unresolved. Compare that with your own priorities: musical style, communication, schedule, cost and the kind of feedback you prefer. If key questions are unanswered, ask them before agreeing to more. A good fit is not simply a coach you admire; it is a working relationship in which the aims, boundaries and next steps are clear enough for you to choose freely.
