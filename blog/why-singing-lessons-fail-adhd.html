Why Singing Lessons Fail People With ADHD

I have ADHD. I also spent years being taught by people who did not, and I know exactly why those lessons did not stick. It was not effort and it was not talent.

The problem is not attention, it is retention

The stereotype says ADHD means you cannot concentrate. In a singing lesson the opposite is usually true. Most ADHD singers I teach concentrate ferociously for an hour, then lose almost all of it somewhere between the lesson and the front door.

Conventional teaching assumes you will remember. You are told something useful, you nod, and you are expected to carry it home in your head and reproduce it on Tuesday. For a lot of brains that works. For mine it never did.

So the failure gets read as not applying yourself, when what actually happened is that the information was handed over in a format your brain does not store.

Nothing in my lessons depends on your memory. After every session you get a link to your own folder containing the recording, a written summary of what we worked on, and a clear path telling you exactly what to practise. It stays there. You can open it three months later when something starts slipping.

Six corrections at once is six corrections wasted

The other thing conventional lessons do is stack. Breathe lower, open the vowel, drop the jaw, lift the palate, support from underneath, and do not forget the words.

Every one of those instructions might be correct. Together they are noise. You end up managing a checklist instead of singing, and the singing gets worse, and then you conclude you are bad at this.

One change. That is all. Make it, feel it, put it in the song, leave the rest for another day.

Why interest matters more than discipline

ADHD brains do not run on discipline, they run on interest. Everybody knows this about themselves and almost nobody applies it to learning an instrument.

If you have an obsession with one artist, one song, one particular sound, that is not a distraction from the work. That is the work. I would far rather build a lesson around the song you cannot stop playing than around an exercise you will abandon by Thursday.

A practice plan that ignores what you actually care about is a practice plan you will not do.

If an explanation does not land, that is my problem. Not evidence that you are slow. I will explain it a different way, then another, until one works. Some people need the feeling, some need the picture, some need the anatomy, some need a daft comparison about drinking through a narrow straw.

What consistency really looks like

You will practise brilliantly for nine days and then not at all for three weeks. I know. Stop building plans that assume otherwise.

Fifteen focused minutes four times a week, sustained over a year, beats an hour a day for a fortnight followed by nothing. The plan that survives a bad week is worth more than a perfect one you abandon.

And when you come back after a gap, you should be able to pick up exactly where you left off rather than guessing. That is a systems problem, not a willpower problem, and it is solvable.

What I would do in your lesson

We would work on one thing, not six. I would watch your face as much as I listen, because tension shows there first. If you have not practised since last time, we get on with the lesson, there is no telling off. And you leave with it written down so Tuesday you is not relying on today you.
