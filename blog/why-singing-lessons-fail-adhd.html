When Singing Lessons Do Not Work for Some People with ADHD

First published 19 September 2026 · Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

People with ADHD do not all learn or organise practice in the same way. A lesson that depends on remembering many instructions may be less useful for some people than one with a clear focus.

When a lesson feels hard to use afterwards

A lesson can feel productive in the room and still be difficult to translate into practice. That gap does not, by itself, tell you why. The instruction may have been too broad, too numerous, or hard to recall; the practice slot may not fit your week; or the musical target may not yet be clear. A singer might also understand a correction immediately but need a different way to retrieve it later. None of those observations establish that ADHD is the cause or that a singer lacks commitment.

ADHD is a developmental disorder diagnosed through healthcare assessment, not a coaching label for distraction or an explanation for every uneven practice week. The NIMH overview of ADHD describes symptoms and diagnosis in health-care terms; NICE guidance likewise addresses assessment and management, not a one-size-fits-all singing lesson. People with ADHD differ, and a singer need not disclose a diagnosis to ask for a different teaching format.

Make the musical target small enough to find again

Take “work on the chorus” and narrow it to a musical event: the first pitch, a rhythm that slips, a consonant that interrupts the line, or the point where the melody changes direction. A coach can sing or play the passage, ask you to repeat it, and listen for what is already stable before choosing one adjustment. The next step might be to sing it once with the accompaniment, then once without it, rather than receiving several unrelated corrections.

For a concrete example, use “Dog Days Are Over” by Florence + The Machine. Choose a short phrase in the verse or refrain, identify where its rhythm or pitch needs attention, and mark the start point in the track. The aim is not to imitate the recording wholesale. A coach can listen to whether the pulse, entry and pitch shape stay clear as you repeat the same small section, and whether a spoken or sung demonstration makes the task easier to understand.

Test retrieval, not just immediate success

Getting a phrase right straight after a demonstration shows one kind of learning; finding it again later is another useful test. After a short break, return to the same entry without replaying the instruction. If it is difficult, add a cue you choose: a written phrase name, a phone reminder, a voice note, a marked lyric sheet, or a starting pitch. Later, cover the prompt and see what you can recall. These are experiments, not prescribed ADHD techniques, and the most useful prompt is the one you actually want to use.

A coach can compare immediate repetition with a return after a pause, or try a different cue, while keeping the musical material constant. They can listen for whether the challenge is remembering where to begin, hearing the pitch, coordinating the rhythm, or applying a technique instruction. Describing the observable difference gives you something specific to adjust. It is more informative than treating a forgotten instruction as proof that you did not care.

Design practice around your actual week

Instead of setting an idealised daily schedule, choose a realistic place and moment for one short action. That might be singing a phrase once before another regular activity, listening to the reference track on a journey, or practising for five focused minutes when you have quiet. If plans regularly change, use a flexible cue rather than turning a missed session into a backlog. Decide in advance what counts as complete: for example, locating the phrase and singing it twice with one agreed focus.

Some people like a written recap; others prefer a short demonstration, a recording, a visual mark, or no extra material. Ask which is useful rather than assuming that a checklist, app or energetic lesson will suit you. A coach can end by asking you to explain the next action in your own words and by checking that the notes are brief enough to use. Change the format if the plan repeatedly stays on the page rather than entering your practice.

Separate musical difficulty from lesson format

Uneven results can have several explanations. A melody may sit in a demanding part of your range; the backing track may mask your pitch; a long explanation may obscure the one change to make; or the lesson may move on before you have tried the adjustment. A contemporary singing coach can listen to pitch, timing, breath, consonants and how a phrase changes across repetitions, then test one variable at a time. They can also ask what you heard and what you want to work on, rather than inferring a cause from behaviour.

Common mistaken assumptions include “if I wanted it enough, I would practise”, “everyone with ADHD needs the same short lesson”, and “a new app will solve the problem”. None follows from the musical evidence. Consistency is affected by ordinary circumstances as well as preferences, and technology is only useful if it reduces friction for you. The fair test is whether the chosen arrangement makes the song task clearer and easier to revisit, not whether it resembles someone else’s routine.

Keep coaching within its role

Voice coaching can adapt instructions, pacing and musical exercises by agreement. It cannot diagnose ADHD, provide clinical treatment, or promise that a particular adjustment will work. You control what you share and can ask to pause, repeat, write something down or try another format. If you want advice about ADHD symptoms or diagnosis, that belongs with an appropriately qualified health professional; a singing lesson should stay focused on the voice and the music.

Before your next session, bring one song and one point where the current process stops helping. Agree one observable musical target, one chosen reminder and a realistic way to revisit it. At the following lesson, check what happened without scoring your motivation: did you find the section, remember the cue and hear a change? Keep what helped, alter what did not, and let the next plan reflect your own experience rather than a stereotype.
