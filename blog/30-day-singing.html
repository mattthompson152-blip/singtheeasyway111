A Month of Singing Practice: What to Assess

Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

A month can help you notice what is becoming more dependable in a song. It cannot promise a fixed transformation or a particular number of new notes.

Pick one task from a song you actually sing

A month-long review works best when you can describe the task in one sentence. Choose a short passage and one observable question: do the words stay clear through the chorus, can you keep the same rhythmic placement on repeated attempts, or does the breath plan let you finish the line without rushing? “Improve my voice” is too broad to assess; “keep the final phrase of ‘Flowers’ by Miley Cyrus clear at the tempo I use” gives you something specific to revisit. Your task can change later, but avoid changing it every day.

Choose a section that is manageable to repeat and relevant to your aim. In ‘Anti-Hero’ by Taylor Swift, you might track whether the consonants remain distinct in a quick lyric. In ‘Someone You Loved’ by Lewis Capaldi, you could notice whether your phrasing changes when you sing with a track versus alone. These are example listening questions, not claims about what every singer should do with those songs. If the song’s original key is unsuitable for you, note your chosen key and keep it consistent when comparing clips.

Make a baseline you can repeat

At the start, record one short attempt or make a written observation if recording does not help you. Note the date, exact section, key or track version, and one thing you will listen for. Keep the device, room, approximate position and accompaniment as similar as practical for later comparisons. A phone recording can serve as a personal reference; it does not have to be a studio-quality document or a public performance.

Do not try to evaluate everything at once. If your task is clear words, listen for intelligibility rather than simultaneously rating pitch, tone, expression and confidence. If it is timing, mark where the phrase sits against the accompaniment. You might write, “I hear the last two words compress before the beat,” which is more useful than “bad take.” The note describes what you noticed without turning a single clip into a judgement about your ability.

Practice the task, then leave room to listen

Choose a repeatable practice slot that fits your life rather than promising yourself a demanding daily schedule. Decide what you will do with the passage and how you will know when to stop. A brief focused session may be enough to test one musical choice; repeating a line many times without a question can make it harder to judge what changed. If you miss a day or a week, resume when practical and record the interruption honestly instead of treating the month as a pass-or-fail challenge.

For example, you might sing the same four bars of ‘Levitating’ by Dua Lipa with the track, then without it, and note what is easier to hear in each version. Or you could compare two ways of entering the chorus in ‘As It Was’ by Harry Styles, changing only the phrasing choice you are examining. The comparison is useful only if you can explain what differed. If the song or accompaniment changes, note it; otherwise those takes are different tasks, not clean evidence of progress.

Use comparable check-ins, not a promised timeline

Revisit the baseline at intervals that are realistic for you, such as once a week, using roughly the same excerpt and setup. Record the date and one observation each time. You might notice that a word stays clearer, that the tempo feels less surprising, or that the original difficulty is unchanged. Any of those is information. A month does not guarantee a particular result, a new range or a fixed transformation, and one recording cannot isolate every reason a performance sounded different.

Listen at a comfortable level and leave a little space between recording and review if you need fresh ears. Compare the clips in a consistent way: same passage, similar playback level and the same question. If one take is much louder or uses a different track, do not treat that alone as improvement. You can write “unclear from these clips” and choose a more useful test rather than forcing a positive or negative conclusion.

Track one task without turning practice into a score

A simple log can include the date, task, what you tried, what you noticed and the next small question. For example: “Chorus, words at the end of the phrase; tried singing with the instrumental; the consonants are easier to hear without it; next time check whether the rhythm still feels steady.” This gives you a record of decisions rather than an invented rating system. A numerical score can look precise without explaining what you heard, so use one only if you know what it means and it helps you make a choice.

If the same difficulty persists, ask what information is missing. Could the key, accompaniment, tempo or recording make the comparison confusing? Is the task too broad, or does it involve a musical detail you cannot assess alone? The article on reliability in songs considers repetition and consistency, while the pitch accuracy guide focuses on listening to pitch in contemporary repertoire.

Review the month and choose what comes next

At the end, compare the first and later notes against the single question you chose. Summarise what seems different, what stayed the same and what you cannot tell. Then decide whether to keep the same task, adjust it or choose a new one. You may find that your practice was more consistent, that a particular section is easier to assess, or that your original method did not answer the question. Those are valid conclusions even without a dramatic before-and-after clip.

Keep the next step modest and specific: repeat the same excerpt under consistent conditions, ask a coach to listen for one detail, or choose another passage that tests the same idea. If the recording conditions changed, label that clearly. The purpose of a 30-day review is to make your observations more comparable and your practice decisions more deliberate, not to meet an outcome deadline or prove that every singer progresses on the same schedule.
