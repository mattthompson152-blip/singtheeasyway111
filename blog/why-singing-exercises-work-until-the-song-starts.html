Why Your Singing Exercises Work Until You Start the Song

First published 4 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

The exercise works.

The note becomes easier. The sound is clearer. The voice feels more responsive, and you repeat the result several times.

Then you start singing the actual song.

Within seconds, the improvement disappears.

The throat tightens again. The vowel becomes difficult. The pitch becomes less stable. The phrase feels exactly as it did before the exercise supposedly fixed it.

This is one of the most common problems I encounter in singing lessons.

It can be deeply frustrating because the singer has just heard proof that the voice can produce a different result. The improvement was real. It was not imagined, and it was not necessarily a lucky accident.

Yet the moment the words, melody and meaning of the song return, the voice goes back to the old habit.

This does not automatically mean the exercise failed.

It usually means that an important part of the learning process is still missing.

The singer has found the coordination in one carefully controlled situation but has not yet learnt how to recognise, reproduce and maintain it inside the considerably more complicated demands of a real song.

An exercise creates a simplified version of the problem

A well-chosen vocal exercise can be extremely useful.

It can temporarily remove many of the demands that make the original phrase difficult:

  • complicated lyrics;
  • rapid consonants;
  • changing vowel shapes;
  • an unfamiliar melody;
  • demanding rhythm;
  • emotional interpretation;
  • anticipation of a difficult note;
  • pressure to perform the entire section correctly;
  • memories of previous unsuccessful attempts.

This creates space for the singer to explore one specific vocal decision.

For example, I might remove the lyrics and ask the singer to produce the melody on one comfortable sound. That can help us discover whether the voice can move through the pitches without the additional demands of articulation.

The singer may suddenly find that the difficult note is available.

That is useful evidence.

However, it does not yet tell us why the original lyric disrupted the coordination. Nor does it prove that the singer can reproduce the improvement once the original musical demands return.

The simplicity that makes the exercise useful is also what makes it different from the song.

The exercise is not the final result. It is a controlled environment in which we can investigate part of the problem.

Producing the sound once is not the same as learning it

A singer may produce a more useful sound while I demonstrate the exercise, play the notes and direct every repetition.

That is progress, but it is only one stage of progress.

There is a considerable difference between:

  • copying a sound;
  • experiencing a better coordination;
  • recognising what changed;
  • reproducing that change with help;
  • reproducing it without help;
  • retaining it when the lyrics return;
  • applying it in another song;
  • recognising when the old habit has returned;
  • knowing how to recover independently.

A lesson can appear successful because the singer briefly sounds better while the teacher is controlling every stage.

That is not enough.

If the singer cannot identify what changed or reproduce the result independently, the improvement may disappear as soon as the lesson ends.

My objective is not to make the singer dependent on my demonstration or constant correction. It is to help the singer understand the decision well enough to recognise and repeat it.

The words can completely change the vocal task

Singing a melody on one comfortable sound is not the same task as singing it with the original words.

Every word introduces movement.

The tongue changes position. The jaw moves. The lips alter their shape. Consonants briefly interrupt or redirect the sound. Vowels continually change the shape of the vocal tract.

A note that feels easy on one sound may become difficult when the original lyric returns.

Suppose a singer can produce a high note comfortably on “ah” but struggles when the lyric places a narrower vowel on the same pitch. The exercise may prove that the pitch itself is available. It does not prove that the singer has found a workable relationship between that pitch and the original vowel.

The problem may also begin before the difficult note.

A consonant in the preceding word may create unnecessary effort. The singer may close the mouth too early. The tongue may prepare for the next syllable before the current note is complete. The jaw may become less responsive because the singer is anticipating the difficult part of the phrase.

By the time the high note arrives, the coordination has already changed.

This is why I do not simply ask whether the singer can hit the note.

I want to know exactly where the phrase starts to become less workable.

Attention changes when the song returns

During a short exercise, the singer may have only one thing to consider.

During a song, attention can move between:

  • remembering the next lyric;
  • following the rhythm;
  • listening to the accompaniment;
  • preparing for a high note;
  • controlling the volume;
  • communicating the meaning;
  • maintaining the desired vocal quality;
  • worrying about repeating a previous mistake.

The singer is no longer completing one simple vocal task. The singer is coordinating several musical and technical demands at the same time.

When attention moves elsewhere, the voice may return to its most familiar habit.

This does not mean that the singer is careless or incapable. It means that the newer coordination is not yet sufficiently established to remain available when attention is divided.

Telling the singer to concentrate harder is rarely a useful solution.

Instead, I reduce the gap between the simple exercise and the full musical phrase.

The meaning of the song can alter the voice

Lyrics do more than change vowels and consonants. They also carry meaning.

A singer may complete an exercise with relative ease, then approach the original lyric with a completely different physical intention.

A forceful lyric may encourage the singer to push. A vulnerable lyric may cause the sound to become deliberately breathy. An anticipated high note may create an attempt to sound powerful before the pitch has even arrived.

These choices may be musically understandable, but they can also reintroduce the coordination the exercise temporarily removed.

This is why technical work cannot remain separate from interpretation.

The question is not simply:

“Can the singer produce the note?”

It is also:

“Can the singer produce the note while communicating the lyric in a way that remains vocally workable?”

The musical intention should be included in the work rather than added only after the technique is supposedly complete.

I build a bridge back into the song

Moving directly from a simple exercise to the complete phrase can be too large a jump.

Instead, I build a bridge between them.

That might involve:

  1. identifying the precise point where the old habit returns;
  2. reducing the phrase to two or three important notes;
  3. singing those notes on one workable sound;
  4. introducing the main vowel from the lyric;
  5. restoring the consonants around that vowel;
  6. adding the preceding word;
  7. returning the fragment to its original rhythm;
  8. rebuilding the complete line;
  9. restoring the intended musical meaning;
  10. testing whether the singer can repeat the decision without prompting.

Each stage adds one of the demands that the original exercise removed.

This allows us to observe where the coordination changes rather than waiting until the whole phrase has failed.

The purpose is not to make the singer approach music mechanically. It is to prevent the actual problem from becoming hidden beneath several simultaneous demands.

A practical example

Imagine that a singer struggles with the final word of a chorus.

The note is not exceptionally high, but the singer consistently reaches it with a tight jaw and an unstable pitch.

We replace the words with one sound, and the note immediately becomes easier.

It would be tempting to conclude that the singer simply needs to practise the exercise more.

But when the lyric returns, the difficulty returns too.

Closer examination reveals that the final word begins with a consonant that causes the singer to close the mouth abruptly. The tongue moves into position early, the jaw becomes less responsive and the vowel is never given enough time to settle.

The high note is not the first problem.

The preparation for the word is the problem.

We might then:

  • separate the vowel from the consonant;
  • sing the vowel on the target pitch;
  • add the consonant without changing the jaw position prematurely;
  • restore the preceding word;
  • return the phrase to its musical rhythm;
  • add the intended delivery;
  • test the complete chorus after a short break.

The exercise opened the door.

The detailed work showed the singer how to walk through it.

More repetitions are not always the answer

If the singer completes the exercise successfully thirty times and loses the result thirty times in the song, another thirty isolated repetitions may not resolve the problem.

The singer may simply become better at performing the exercise.

Repetition can create a false sense of progress. The exercise starts to feel familiar, predictable and comfortable. The singer may interpret that familiarity as evidence that the vocal problem has been solved.

Then the song reveals that the improvement exists only under the exact conditions of the exercise.

The singer needs opportunities to make decisions, notice errors and recover rather than waiting for the teacher to judge every attempt.

Practice needs to resemble the result you want

Practice should eventually contain the skills and conditions required by the final task.

This does not mean that every practice attempt should involve singing the complete song from beginning to end.

It means that the exercise must eventually reconnect with the actual musical demand.

If the problem occurs on a specific lyric, the lyric must return.

If the difficulty appears at performance volume, that dynamic must eventually be tested.

If the singer loses the coordination while communicating a particular meaning, that intention must become part of the work.

If the improvement exists only on one exercise sound, the practice has not yet become sufficiently specific.

The singer has to recognise the difference

One of the most important questions I ask is not:

“Did that sound better?”

It is:

“What did you notice that would help you find it again?”

The singer’s answer may refer to sound, effort, vowel shape, musical intention or a personal sensation.

The language does not have to sound technically impressive.

It has to be useful to that singer.

One singer may recognise that the vowel felt less spread. Another may notice that the note arrived without a sudden increase in volume. Someone else may hear that the phrase remained connected.

These observations create usable reference points.

The best cue is the one that helps the individual reproduce the right decision.

A good exercise should disappear into the music

The final purpose of an exercise is not to create a flawless exercise.

It is to help the singer make a more dependable musical choice.

Eventually, the exercise should become almost invisible. The coordination it helped establish should remain while the singer’s attention returns to the lyric, story and performance.

The singer is no longer trying to insert an exercise into the song.

The singer is simply singing the song with a better organised voice.

Your voice may not need another exercise

If you have collected exercises that work briefly but never change your singing, ask:

  • At what exact moment does the improvement disappear?
  • What changes when the lyrics return?
  • Does the problem begin before the difficult note?
  • Can I recognise the successful coordination without copying somebody?
  • Have I practised the actual musical demand?
  • Can I recover when the old habit returns?

The exercise may already have shown you that the voice can do something different.

The next task is learning how to carry that difference into the music.
