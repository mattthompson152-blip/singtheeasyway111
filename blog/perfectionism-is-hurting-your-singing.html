Perfectionism and Singing: When Monitoring Interrupts Music

First published 14 September 2026 · Updated 2 November 2026

By Matt Thompson

Wanting to sing well is not the problem. Difficulty begins when judging every sound in real time leaves no attention for the next word or musical idea.

Notice what your standards make you attend to

High standards can support careful singing, but an all-or-nothing review can make every take feel unusable. The question is not whether you should care about quality; it is what you listen for and whether your review gives you a next action. You might replay a small mistake while missing a clear phrase, compare a first attempt with a polished recording, or keep changing a vowel without deciding what sound you want. Those are review habits to observe, not a diagnosis.

Perfectionism can mean different things in everyday conversation. A coach should not assume that a singer who revises carefully has a mental-health problem, or claim that one recording habit caused a difficulty. Instead, identify the standard you are applying: accurate pitch, clear words, a particular tone, or a take with no audible variation. Some standards are useful for a specific goal; others may be impossible or irrelevant to the song you are learning.

Use one question per listen

Choose a short section and decide what you want the recording to tell you before pressing play. Listen once for timing, once for pitch, or once for whether the lyric is understandable. Stop after the chosen question has an answer. If you hear something to change, describe it in an actionable way: “the phrase speeds up after the pause” is more useful than “that sounded bad”. A contemporary coach can listen alongside you and distinguish an audible feature from a preference about style.

Take “When We Were Young” by Adele as a popular-song example. Choose a line that is comfortable in your range, record one take, and review it first for the agreed target—perhaps where the pulse settles after a breath. Do not use the studio recording as a demand to reproduce its production, vocal colour or every expressive detail. A coach can help decide whether the musical issue is timing, breath, key or simply a stylistic choice you want to make differently.

Separate a useful second take from endless retakes

Repeating a phrase can help when each attempt tests a clear adjustment. It becomes less informative when you make many takes without changing a specific choice or when each replay expands the list of flaws. Set a small boundary: two or three attempts at one passage, with one adjustment between them, then take a break or move on. You can choose a different limit. The number is a practical option, not a clinical rule.

For the Adele example, make one take at your comfortable tempo, then a second with a marked breath or a clearer count-in. Compare only the agreed musical feature. If the change is not helpful, return to the first version or ask the coach for another idea. A coach can listen for whether the adjustment changes pitch, timing, tone or effort, and can remind you of what is already working. You do not need to keep a recording that makes practice less useful.

Distinguish a standard from a preference

Some goals are measurable enough to guide practice: entering on the cue, sustaining a note for the phrase, or keeping the rhythm with the track. Other judgements—“my voice must sound impressive” or “that take must be perfect”—may conceal a more specific preference. Ask what “good” would sound like in this song and whether the chosen standard serves its style. You may prefer a conversational sound over a large one, or a slightly varied phrase over mechanical consistency.

When coaching “When We Were Young”, for instance, the useful target might be a steady entrance rather than an exact copy of Adele’s recorded intensity. A coach can offer two versions of a phrase and ask which better fits your intended meaning, while checking that both are comfortable. Musical choices need not be ranked as universally correct. The singer’s taste and the practical needs of the song both matter.

Make recording review optional and bounded

Recording is only one source of feedback. You might ask a coach to describe what they hear live, use a keyboard to check a starting pitch, or sing the passage without any review. If recording is useful, decide in advance whether to save it, how long to keep it and whether you want to share it. You can delete it or decline recording. No singer has to turn every rehearsal into an archive for self-assessment.

Common assumptions include that more listening always improves a take, that discomfort means the recording is accurate, and that a technically cleaner version is automatically the more expressive one. None is guaranteed. A recording captures one moment and one setup. Listen for the detail you chose, then return to singing or stop. If reviewing audio causes persistent distress, a coach can respect that boundary and work through another method; they should not treat it as a condition to overcome.

Replace the verdict with a next-step note

After a practice, write one observation and one next action. For example: “I found the starting pitch; next time I will keep the same tempo through the last two bars.” Also note one thing that already worked, such as a clear consonant or a comfortable key. This is not forced positivity. It prevents a single flaw from erasing the useful evidence and gives the next session a practical starting point.

If perfectionistic worries feel severe, persistent or affect other areas of life, that is beyond singing coaching. A coach can keep the work focused on a song, a sound and your chosen standard, but cannot provide mental-health treatment. In a lesson, agree the recording question, set a review limit and decide what counts as enough information. The aim is deliberate attention—not a promise that every take will satisfy every expectation.
